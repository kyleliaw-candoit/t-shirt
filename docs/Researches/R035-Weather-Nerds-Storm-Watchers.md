## Final Community Research Report

# 1. Executive Summary

A meaningful identity exists among people who attend closely to forecasts, radar, models, clouds, storms, measurements, and local weather beyond practical necessity. **Weather nerd/geek**, **weather enthusiast**, **storm spotter**, and **storm chaser** are related but not equivalent. “Storm watcher” is understandable but less precise as a participant-native umbrella.

The broad identity can be practiced safely from home through radar/model analysis, backyard instruments, photography, weather logging, and public education. Trained SKYWARN spotters report ground truth; storm chasers travel to intercept storms; professional meteorologists have an occupational identity. These roles should never be collapsed into “tornado hunter.”

T-shirts fit home analysis, classes, conferences, outreach, club activity, and calm-weather gathering. Field participation requires weather-specific layers, high visibility, sturdy footwear, rain/cold/sun protection, communications, and safety equipment; a T-shirt is only one layer.

---

# 2. Core Identity

The identity is built around sustained attention to atmospheric conditions and the satisfaction of understanding what the sky is doing. Participants recognize changes in clouds, pressure, temperature, wind, radar signatures, forecast discussions, and model behavior that most people notice only when plans are disrupted.

Emotional rewards include anticipation, pattern recognition, awe, scientific learning, documenting rare phenomena, public service, and becoming the person friends ask when weather turns serious.

---

# 3. Iteration One — Breadth Findings

Evidence spans NWS SKYWARN training, storm-spotter networks, CoCoRaHS-type observation, Stormtrack and chasing communities, amateur radio, weather forums, model discussion, photography, local storm teams, conferences, and weather apparel.

The umbrella covers many risk levels and motivations. Most enthusiasts do not chase severe storms, and many trained spotters report from fixed safe locations. Community size or cohesion is not required for the strong personal identity visible in habitual forecast and radar use.

---

# 4. Primary Identity Drivers

1. **Pattern recognition:** reading the atmosphere, radar, models, and local clues.
2. **Anticipation:** following setups and forecast evolution before an event.
3. **Scientific curiosity:** learning dynamics, cloud structure, and measurement.
4. **Documentation:** photographs, video, logs, rain/snow totals, and reports.
5. **Public service:** timely, accurate spotter reporting and preparedness education.
6. **Awe and intensity:** experiencing storms and rare atmospheric phenomena.
7. **Local expertise:** knowing how terrain and seasonal patterns affect a place.

---

# 5. Participation Archetypes

- **Forecast / Model Watcher:** compares guidance, outlooks, discussions, and radar from home.
- **Backyard Observer:** runs instruments, logs precipitation, or photographs clouds.
- **Trained Storm Spotter:** follows reporting criteria and sends ground truth to authorities.
- **Storm Chaser:** travels to observe or document storms, with significant route and safety demands.
- **Weather Communicator:** explains risks and preparedness to a local or online audience.
- **Phenomenon Specialist:** focuses on tornadoes, snow, tropical weather, lightning, aurora, or clouds.

---

# 6. Language, Rituals, and Status Signals

Common language includes **outlook**, **watch**, **warning**, **mesoscale discussion**, **convective**, **CAPE**, **shear**, **hook echo**, **velocity couplet**, **debris signature**, **wall cloud**, **shelf cloud**, **supercell**, **MCS**, **nowcast**, **bust**, **ground truth**, and model names or run times.

Rituals include checking morning guidance, watching forecast discussions, refreshing model runs, monitoring radar, charging devices, attending annual spotter training, preparing a field kit, documenting clouds, reporting threshold events, and conducting a post-event review.

Competence is signaled by accurate terminology, calibrated uncertainty, safety judgment, reliable reports, local knowledge, and willingness to defer to official warnings. Dramatic footage without safe practice is contested, not automatic status.

---

# 7. Frustrations and Fault Lines

- Forecast busts, model disagreement, radar gaps, poor visibility, late-night events, and equipment or connectivity failure.
- Chasers versus spotters; field intercept versus home analysis; professionals versus amateurs; scientific explanation versus engagement-driven hype.
- Unsafe driving, road congestion, stopping in traffic, trespass, interfering with responders, and filming victims are major ethical concerns.
- “Certified meteorologist” implications and official-looking branding can mislead the public.
- Tornado fixation can crowd out floods, heat, lightning, winter weather, and other more common hazards.
- Climate change discussion and public misinformation can create politicized conflict.

---

# 8. Community Cohesion and Expression

Community is networked through NWS offices and trainings, amateur-radio groups, spotter teams, forums, local storm organizations, social media, weather conferences, and professional-amateur overlap. Trained reporting provides formal structure for one subgroup; enthusiasts outside it remain a real identity.

Public signaling includes radar screenshots, weather-station displays, vehicle decals, SKYWARN identifiers, storm-team apparel, photography, maps, stickers, and weather jokes. Official program marks and professional titles require careful authorized use.

---

# 9. Participation Utility / Apparel Fit

T-shirts are normal for home monitoring, training, meetings, conferences, outreach, calm-weather photography, and warm-weather field preparation. Storm teams and spotting groups use shirts for affiliation and fundraising.

Active field participation makes functional clothing primary: rain shells, insulated layers, sun protection, sturdy waterproof footwear, high-visibility items, gloves, and season-specific gear. Severe weather is not made safe by identity apparel. Vehicle readiness, escape routes, situational awareness, communications, and avoiding flooded roads or lightning exposure matter more.

Secondary value includes scientific aesthetics, conversation (“ask me about the forecast”), emotional awe, event documentation, community-service pride, and commemorative storm or snowfall references.

---

# 10. Cultural / Outsider Risks

Never glorify unsafe chasing, frame disaster damage as entertainment, or use victims and destroyed communities as aesthetic props. Spotter and warning language must be exact; a watch is not a warning, and visual clues are not a license for untrained certainty.

Avoid suggesting affiliation with NOAA/NWS, emergency management, or a professional credential without authorization. Do not mock people who take shelter or imply risk-taking proves authenticity.

Weather varies regionally. Tornado-centric US language does not represent tropical, mountain, marine, polar, monsoon, or other weather cultures worldwide.

---

# 11. Boundary Assessment

**Rename and segment.** Use **Weather Enthusiasts** or **Weather Nerds** as the broad identity. Treat **trained storm spotters**, **storm chasers**, **weather-station observers**, and phenomenon-specific groups as distinct practices. “Storm watchers” overweights severe weather and does not communicate training or mobility.

Professional meteorologists, emergency managers, and broadcast weather staff are adjacent occupational identities even when they also self-identify as weather nerds.

# 16. Methodology Notes / Gaps

US severe-weather and SKYWARN evidence is overrepresented. The broader everyday-weather identity, international terminology, winter/tropical/marine specialists, women and minority chasers, and people who participate solely through home observation need deeper coverage.

Apparel evidence shows established group and enthusiast products, not systematic use during hazardous field conditions. Safety guidance should always defer to current local authorities.

---

# 17. Final Assessment

A meaningful weather-enthusiast identity exists through habitual atmospheric attention, specialized language, observation, anticipation, documentation, and sometimes public service. Storm spotting and chasing are important subgroups, not synonyms for the whole identity.

For Strategy review, T-shirts fit safe indoor, educational, social, and some fair-weather contexts; protective functional layers dominate hazardous field participation. Credibility depends on accuracy, humility, safety, and respect for affected communities.
