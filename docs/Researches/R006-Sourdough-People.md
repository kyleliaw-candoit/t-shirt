# 1. Executive Summary

Sourdough People represent a real and behaviorally rich identity, not merely a consumer category of people who enjoy sourdough bread.

The strongest shared identity mechanism is:

> **treating sourdough as an ongoing practice worth maintaining, learning, troubleshooting, optimizing, and caring about beyond simply obtaining bread.**

The clearest participation continuum is:

> **eat sourdough → bake occasionally → maintain a starter → organize routines around the process → identify as a sourdough baker/person**

This distinction matters because:

> **consumption ≠ participation ≠ identity**

Identity strengthens when the practice acquires routines, specialized knowledge, emotional attachment, equipment, failure stories, personal preferences, and a sense of authorship over the process.

The starter is especially identity-rich because it behaves almost like a dependent system requiring care. Feeding schedules, discard management, rise behavior, temperature, timing, and revival after neglect all create recurring behavior and humor.

After three iterations, Sourdough People receive:

> **Community Score: 89/100**

and:

> **Creative Leverage: 20/20**

Founder Advantage is:

> **2/10**

The identity shows strong public-expression potential, extensive insider language, recurring rituals, and clear independent merchandise evidence. Community cohesion is moderate-to-high among active bakers, but participation in an organized community is not required for strong identity.

# 2. Core Identity

A useful working definition is:

> **Sourdough People are individuals for whom maintaining, baking, learning, experimenting with, or caring for sourdough has become a recurring personal practice rather than merely a way to obtain bread.**

The identity is process-centered.

Someone who simply likes eating sourdough may have a strong product preference without having a sourdough identity.

Identity becomes more visible when someone begins to:

- maintain a starter,
- learn fermentation behavior,
- troubleshoot failed loaves,
- acquire specialized equipment,
- discuss hydration and timing,
- compare crumb structure,
- experiment with flour or inclusions,
- share starter or bread with others,
- or organize routines around fermentation.

The process creates authorship: the person is not merely consuming bread but producing, maintaining, and improving something through repeated attention.

---

# 3. Primary Identity Drivers

## 3.1 Process Ownership

The strongest driver is ownership of the process.

Sourdough requires repeated intervention rather than one-time consumption. The baker maintains an ongoing system involving:

- starter feeding,
- fermentation timing,
- temperature,
- flour choice,
- hydration,
- shaping,
- proofing,
- scoring,
- and baking.

This creates repeated opportunities for skill, judgment, and personal preference to become identity.

---

## 3.2 Starter Caretaking

The starter is a distinctive identity object.

It is maintained over time, can be shared or inherited, may be named, can be neglected and revived, and often becomes the subject of anthropomorphic humor.

Common identity-rich behaviors include:

- naming the starter,
- worrying whether it is alive or healthy,
- remembering or forgetting feedings,
- discussing starter age,
- sharing starter with friends,
- reviving neglected starter,
- and treating starter maintenance as a recurring household responsibility.

The starter therefore functions as both a technical tool and a recurring cultural object.

---

## 3.3 Skill and Mastery

Sourdough rewards accumulated judgment.

Experienced bakers learn to interpret:

- rise and fall behavior,
- dough strength,
- fermentation readiness,
- temperature effects,
- hydration,
- shaping quality,
- crust development,
- and crumb structure.

This creates a progression from recipe-following toward tacit expertise.

Mastery can become a status signal, but the community also contains substantial beginner support and normalization of failure.

---

## 3.4 Creative Experimentation

Sourdough supports creative variation through:

- flour blends,
- inclusions,
- decorative scoring,
- shapes,
- discard recipes,
- pizza,
- pancakes,
- crackers,
- pastries,
- and other fermented foods.

This broadens the identity beyond one loaf format while preserving the underlying process orientation.

---

## 3.5 Slow-Living and Handmade Values

For some participants, sourdough sits inside a broader lifestyle involving:

- cooking from scratch,
- homesteading,
- local food,
- fermentation,
- handmade craft,
- self-sufficiency,
- and slower domestic routines.

This is an adjacent identity layer rather than a requirement for sourdough participation.

---

# 4. Participation Archetypes

These archetypes overlap substantially and should not be treated as separate communities.

## Casual Home Baker

Enjoys sourdough as a recurring home activity without pursuing deep technical optimization.

## Starter Caretaker

Forms strong attachment to maintaining and preserving the starter itself.

## Process Optimizer

Pays close attention to hydration, temperature, timing, fermentation, shaping, crumb, and repeatability.

## Creative Baker

Experiments with inclusions, scoring, shapes, discard recipes, and presentation.

## Traditionalist / Authenticity-Oriented Baker

Cares strongly about natural fermentation, ingredient choices, and what qualifies as genuine sourdough.

## Slow-Living / Homestead-Adjacent Participant

Connects sourdough with a broader handmade, self-sufficient, or from-scratch lifestyle.

- monitoring rise behavior,
- timing fermentation,
- shaping and scoring,

- sharing starter,
- and finding uses for discard.

These repeated practices create strong insider recognition and humor.

# 6. Identity Fault Lines

## 6.1 Casual / Pragmatic ↔ Technical / Optimization-Oriented

Some bakers want reliable good bread with minimal complexity.

Others treat fermentation, temperature, hydration, flour, crumb, and timing almost experimentally.

This is a meaningful participation-depth difference but does not currently justify separate research communities.

---

## 6.2 Traditional / Authentic ↔ Flexible / Shortcut-Friendly

Participants differ over what counts as “real” sourdough and how much flexibility is acceptable around commercial yeast, simplified methods, or alternative techniques.

This can become a status or authenticity debate.

---

## 6.3 Bread-Centered ↔ Broader Sourdough Lifestyle

Some participants primarily want great bread.

Others expand into discard cooking, pizza, crackers, pastries, fermentation, cottage baking, local grains, or broader from-scratch lifestyles.

The overlap remains substantial enough to treat these as one broad research identity.

---

## 6.4 Outcome ↔ Process

For some participants, success is primarily the quality of the finished loaf.

For deeper hobbyists, maintaining and mastering the process becomes part of the enjoyment and identity.

---

# 7. Community Cohesion

## Final Assessment

> **Moderate-to-high among active bakers**

Sourdough communities provide substantial peer learning through troubleshooting, recipe exchange, technique discussion, failure analysis, and beginner support.

However, a person does not need to participate in online forums, baking clubs, or social groups to strongly identify as a sourdough baker.

Therefore:

> **community participation is useful evidence of identity, but it is not a prerequisite for identity strength.**

---

# 8. Identity Expression and Signaling

Identity-expression potential is very strong because the hobby naturally generates recognizable behavioral humor and insider shorthand.

Existing identity signaling commonly revolves around:

- needing to feed the starter,
- starter age,
- starter names,
- discard guilt,
- failed loaves,
- being in a “sourdough era,”
- “sourdough starter club” framing,
- technical process vocabulary,
- and obsessive baking behavior.

These expressions work because they communicate behavior, not merely bread imagery.

The strongest identity reaction is likely to come from recognition of the process itself:

> **“That is exactly what I do.”**

# 9. Commercial Validation

Independent sourdough identity merchandise is clearly present in the market.

Observable merchandise themes include:

- sourdough starter clubs,
- feeding-the-starter humor,
- technical process lists,
- bread and starter puns,
- sourdough-era language,
- and gifts explicitly targeted at sourdough bakers.

This is important because the merchandise is not tied to an official franchise, celebrity, or source brand.

It provides direct evidence that participants may purchase products whose value comes primarily from signaling sourdough identity.

The category appears commercially real but smaller and less proven than the most saturated identity-merchandise markets.

# 15. Methodology Notes

R006 reinforces several established methodology principles.

First:

> **ownership or consumption does not equal identity.**

Identity becomes much stronger when recurring practice, specialized knowledge, responsibility, and self-recognition develop around the activity.

Second, R006 demonstrates that a maintenance object can become an identity anchor. The starter is not simply equipment; recurring care creates ritual, humor, language, and emotional attachment.

Third, community cohesion and identity strength remain distinct. Active sourdough communities are useful sources of culture and knowledge, but strong sourdough identity can exist outside organized participation.

No methodology change is required based on R006.

Sourdough People constitute a strong identity centered on process ownership, starter care, skill development, experimentation, and repeated ritual.

The identity is highly expressive and creatively rich.

Its most important common denominator is not bread consumption.

It is:

> **caring enough about the sourdough process to maintain, learn, troubleshoot, personalize, and repeatedly participate in it.**
