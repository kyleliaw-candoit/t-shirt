# 1. Executive Summary

Pottery and ceramics support a recognizable maker identity rooted in transforming clay through practiced hand skill, material knowledge, repeated failure, heat, and time. Participants commonly call themselves **potters**, **ceramic artists**, **ceramicists**, or simply **clay people**. The label chosen often signals practice: “potter” tends to foreground vessels and functional ware, while “ceramic artist” can include sculpture, installation, conceptual work, and nonfunctional forms.

The strongest shared identity mechanism is not ownership of finished ceramics. It is the experience of learning how clay behaves and accepting that no piece is finished until it survives drying, firing, and glaze outcomes. Makers recognize one another through process language, clay-covered routines, studio etiquette, tool preferences, firing knowledge, and stories of collapse, cracking, warping, glaze failure, or unexpected kiln success.

The umbrella is coherent at the material/process level, but it contains meaningful practice distinctions: wheel throwing versus handbuilding; functional pottery versus sculptural ceramics; hobbyist versus production potter; community-studio member versus home-studio maker; and formally trained artist versus self-taught participant. These are important fault lines but do not require five separate identities for broad research.

# 2. Core Identity and Self-Language

A useful working definition is:

> **People for whom making with ceramic clay is a recurring practice, source of skill, emotional reward, and recognizable part of self.**

Common self-labels include potter, ceramicist, ceramic artist, clay artist, wheel thrower, handbuilder, hobby potter, studio potter, production potter, and maker. “Pottery lover” is broader and may include buyers who do not make. “Ceramics maker” is understandable but appears less native than “potter” or “ceramic artist.”

A participation continuum is:

> takes a class → returns for open studio → builds process competence → develops preferred forms/materials → joins or builds a studio practice → teaches, exhibits, sells, or organizes life around clay

Formal credentials are not required for identity, but safety knowledge and process competence matter strongly when work is fired in shared kilns or sold for food use.

# 3. Iteration One — Breadth Findings

Evidence across community studios, NCECA, Ceramic Arts Network, maker forums, studio policies, and marketplace behavior shows:

- durable self-identification around clay and making;
- recurring physical rituals: wedging, centering, pulling, trimming, drying, glazing, firing, reclaiming, and cleaning;
- shared studios that create repeated contact, norms, and conflict;
- deep subgroup structure without loss of a common material core;
- abundant identity apparel and gift language built around tools, mud, kilns, wheels, and process failures;
- substantial participation spending on classes, memberships, clay, tools, kilns, glazes, and firing that must be distinguished from signaling-merchandise evidence.

# 4. Primary Identity Drivers

## 4.1 Transformation and Making

Participants turn an unformed material into a durable object. The sequence from wet clay to fired work makes authorship physically legible and supports pride in “I made this.”

## 4.2 Embodied Skill

Clay exposes technique immediately. Centering, wall thickness, joins, handles, compression, drying, glaze application, and kiln choices create a long competence ladder. Experienced makers can often read process evidence in a finished piece.

## 4.3 Uncertainty and Kiln Outcomes

Drying and firing introduce delayed risk. A maker can do extensive work and still receive cracks, pinholes, crawling, warping, glaze runs, or a failed kiln load. “Kiln gods” humor and kiln-opening anticipation arise from this shared lack of total control.

## 4.4 Material and Sensory Reward

Clay is tactile, repetitive, and absorbing. Participants describe the studio as restorative or focusing, while also rejecting romantic language that hides the real practice, cleanup, dust hazards, and technical knowledge involved.

## 4.5 Functional Intimacy

For functional potters, cups, bowls, plates, and vessels enter daily life. A handmade mug can carry the maker’s mark, hand, glaze decisions, and relationship to the user.

## 4.6 Community and Solitary Practice

Making can be solitary, but community studios, classes, firings, sales, guilds, and conferences create strong recurring peer contact. Studio access is itself a major structural feature because kilns, wheels, ventilation, and glaze systems are expensive and shared rules matter.

# 5. Participation Archetypes

- **Beginner / class potter:** learning basic process and studio norms.
- **Community-studio regular:** sustained practice through memberships and open studio.
- **Wheel thrower:** centers identity around thrown forms and wheel competence.
- **Handbuilder:** builds through slabs, coils, pinch forms, molds, or sculpture.
- **Functional potter:** prioritizes usable ware, ergonomics, durability, and food safety.
- **Ceramic artist / sculptor:** prioritizes expressive, conceptual, or nonfunctional work.
- **Production potter:** repeats forms efficiently and may sell at markets or wholesale.
- **Home-studio maker:** manages personal tools, clay, glaze, ventilation, and sometimes a kiln.
- **Educator / technician:** gains status through teaching, firing knowledge, troubleshooting, and safe shared-studio management.

These archetypes overlap.

# 6. Shared Language, Rituals, and Status Signals

Insider language includes wedge, reclaim, grog, slip, score, greenware, leather hard, bone dry, bisque, cone, reduction, oxidation, glaze fit, crawling, crazing, pinholing, dunting, kiln wash, underglaze, bat, rib, trimming, throwing, pulling a handle, centering, compression, reclaim, seconds, and maker’s mark.

Repeated rituals include reclaiming clay; wrapping work to control drying; checking pieces for leather-hard timing; trimming foot rings; test tiles; waxing or cleaning bottoms; loading and waiting on kilns; opening a firing; evaluating “seconds”; signing work; studio cleanup; and photographing or selling finished pieces.

Competence is signaled through consistent forms, even walls, reliable joins, controlled drying, glaze/firing fluency, kiln-loading judgment, food-safety knowledge, troubleshooting, teaching, and the ability to make deliberate rather than accidental results. Expensive tools alone do not confer status.

---

# 7. Frustrations and Tensions

Recurring tensions include:

- kiln failures and unpredictable glaze outcomes;
- limited shelf, kiln, or open-studio access;
- studio members who do not clean or follow clay/firing rules;
- the cost of classes, memberships, firing, and equipment;
- beginners selling before understanding food safety or durability;
- copying another living maker’s distinctive work;
- wheel throwing receiving more social attention than handbuilding;
- formal training versus self-taught practice;
- production efficiency versus artistic experimentation;
- gatekeeping by experienced members, and misinformation from confident beginners.

# 8. Community Cohesion and Public Signaling

**Community Cohesion: High in shared studios and professional/educational networks; moderate across the full identity.**

Community studios produce repeated face-to-face interaction and practical interdependence. NCECA, guilds, workshops, ceramics programs, sales, online forums, and supplier ecosystems connect makers beyond one studio. Home-studio potters can remain strongly identified without frequent social participation.

Public signaling appears through maker accounts, studio membership, market booths, makers’ marks, process videos, clay-tool imagery, pottery tattoos, aprons, tote bags, stickers, and extensive pottery apparel. Existing language includes “Kiln It,” “Pot Dealer,” “Seize the Clay,” “I Support Revolutions,” and highly specific wheel/kiln jokes. Marketplace claims were treated as directional rather than audited sales.

# 9. Participation Utility / Apparel Fit

An ordinary T-shirt naturally belongs in many pottery settings, but makers often treat studio clothes as expendable workwear.

Participant discussions repeatedly recommend comfortable, washable, short-sleeved shirts or old T-shirts, often under a split apron. Clay, especially darker bodies and some colorants, can stain; wheel throwing leaves clay on the lap and torso; and makers avoid clothing they care about. Coveralls, dedicated shoes, towels, aprons, gloves, and respirators appear depending on task. Dry material handling, sanding, and glaze mixing require safety controls that a T-shirt does not provide.

Observations:

- T-shirts are normal during wheel throwing, handbuilding, classes, and open studio.
- The studio context favors washable, comfortable shirts that can get dirty.
- An identity shirt worn during participation may acquire stains and wear as part of its use, but a premium or light-colored shirt may be protected or avoided.
- Conferences, studio sales, guild events, workshops, teaching, and member gatherings are natural clean-apparel occasions.
- Studio/member shirts and ceramics-network apparel provide direct evidence of peer-facing use.

# 10. Cultural Sensitivities / Outsider-Writing Risks

Ceramics contains culturally specific traditions, forms, symbols, and techniques. Treating Indigenous pottery, Japanese tea ceramics, Chinese traditions, Mexican ceramic lineages, or other living cultural practices as generic visual vocabulary risks misattribution and appropriation. Copying a contemporary maker’s recognizable surface design is also a live community concern.

Outsider writing should avoid portraying pottery as effortless therapy, mystical “mud magic,” or only a wine-night activity. Humor about failure is common, but safety, technical practice, and artistic authorship are taken seriously.

---

# 11. Identity Fault Lines and Boundary Assessment

The proposed umbrella is usable, but its name should be refined to **Potters / Ceramic Artists**. This better reflects native self-language and preserves both functional and sculptural practice.

Important internal distinctions:

- pottery / vessels ↔ ceramic art / sculpture;
- wheel throwing ↔ handbuilding;
- hobbyist ↔ production/professional practice;
- communal studio ↔ private studio;
- formally trained ↔ self-taught.

These differences shape vocabulary and status but retain a strong shared clay-process core. “Pottery lovers” who only purchase work are adjacent, not part of the maker identity. Paint-your-own-pottery customers are also adjacent unless they develop an ongoing making practice.

---

# 12. Adjacent Identities

Adjacent identities include ceramic collectors, sculptors, illustrators, craft-fair sellers, art teachers, interior/design people, home cooks who value handmade ware, kiln technicians, and other craft makers. They should not be assumed interchangeable with potters.

# 16. Methodology Notes and Evidence Gaps

Online pottery communities overrepresent highly engaged makers and studio conflict. Community-studio evidence may underrepresent home-studio and culturally rooted makers. Merchandise abundance is clear, but unit sales and buyer composition were not audited. More direct observation of studio attire across climates and disciplines would improve the Participation Utility section but is unlikely to change the core finding that ordinary shirts are normal, expendable base clothing.

---

# 17. Final Assessment

Potters / Ceramic Artists constitute a coherent maker identity formed through tactile practice, technical learning, delayed uncertainty, visible authorship, and shared studio realities. The umbrella should retain both functional and artistic practice while excluding passive pottery appreciation. The strongest cross-cutting observation is:

> **Clay identity is built as much by learning to manage failure and process as by producing beautiful finished objects.**
