# MVV-001 — Landing-Page Product Image Standard

## Purpose

Define the presentation rules for the standardized primary product images used on the Worth the Detour MVV #1 landing page.

The objective is to make the 11 products feel credible, coherent, and comparable while preserving the truth of the product being evaluated.

## Governing rule: product truth is fixed; merchandising presentation is flexible

Every LP product image must remain true to **two immutable product facts**:

1. **The intended Printful garment / fabric / fit.** The image must represent the actual garment specification intended for production. Do not substitute a different blank, fabric weight, neckline, sleeve length, drape, silhouette, texture, or fit merely because another mockup looks better.
2. **The locked T-shirt design.** Artwork, copy, typography, illustration, color relationships, print placement, relative print scale, and other locked design decisions must not be redesigned or materially altered for the LP image.

If an image cannot preserve both conditions, it is not acceptable as the standardized LP product image.

## Locked-design fidelity requirements

Every LP product image must be checked against the **corresponding locked design source for that exact `design_id`**. The locked source is the authority for what is printed on the garment.

The LP presentation must preserve, as closely as the intended garment and natural drape allow:

- the artwork itself and all wording;
- typography and illustration character;
- print colors and color relationships;
- artwork composition and internal proportions;
- **relative print size on the garment**;
- **vertical and horizontal print placement on the garment**;
- the intended relationship between the printed design and major garment landmarks such as collar, chest, side seams, and hem.

Do not enlarge, shrink, raise, lower, re-center, stretch, compress, or otherwise recompose the print simply to make the product image look more dramatic, readable, premium, or balanced.

Normal perspective, fabric drape, and mild natural folds may create small apparent differences, but the customer-facing image should still represent substantially the same print scale and placement as the locked source.

Before approval, the Creative Director should compare the proposed LP image directly against the locked source rather than relying on memory or a prior generated mockup.

## Garment-material fidelity requirements

The garment should read like the intended production blank rather than a stylized substitute. For the current Comfort Colors 1717 baseline, the LP image should preserve a substantial garment-dyed cotton-jersey character with natural softness and lived-in drape.

Avoid generated surface treatments that make the shirt appear embossed, jacquard, woven, quilted, unusually fuzzy, glossy, paper-like, or otherwise materially different from the intended cotton jersey. Natural cotton texture, stitching, wrinkles, and soft tonal variation are acceptable when they remain believable for the production garment.

## Background and focal-hierarchy requirements

Background context may establish the Worth the Detour world, but it must remain **visually subordinate to the garment and printed design**.

For primary LP product images, especially on mobile:

- use shallow depth of field or equivalent soft defocus when the background contains recognizable objects or environmental detail;
- keep background contrast, sharpness, texture, and visual clutter materially lower than the shirt;
- avoid multiple crisp props, signs, frames, furniture edges, high-contrast objects, or readable background text competing with the design;
- background objects may be recognizable as atmosphere, but should not invite inspection before the garment does;
- do not rely on a busy antique-shop scene merely because it feels on-brand; product inspection takes priority over environmental storytelling;
- if the viewer's eye is pulled away from the shirt or print, simplify, darken, soften, crop, or further defocus the background before approval.

The intended hierarchy is: **printed design first, garment second, merchandising context third**.

## Allowed merchandising styling

Presentation may be adjusted when the underlying product remains truthful. Appropriate examples include:

- rolling sleeves;
- tying or lightly tucking a shirt when appropriate to the intended styling;
- arranging natural fabric folds;
- changing crop, framing, orientation, or background;
- using restrained props or accessories beside the garment;
- cleaning up stray objects or neighboring garments from a source image;
- normalizing lighting and presentation so the assortment feels like one coherent mock store.

These changes are merchandising choices, not product-design changes.

## Disallowed styling changes

Do not use styling to make the customer believe they are evaluating a materially different product. In particular, do not:

- change the apparent garment blank or fabric character;
- materially change neckline, sleeve length, garment weight, silhouette, or fit;
- alter the locked artwork, wording, type, illustration, colors, or composition;
- materially change the intended print size or placement on the garment;
- add effects that make the print appear embroidered, puff-printed, washed, distressed, glossy, dimensional, or otherwise different from the intended production treatment unless that treatment is actually part of the locked product;
- obscure important parts of the design with folds, props, accessories, or cropping.

## Assortment-level fairness

The 11 standardized primary images do not need to be visually identical, but they should feel comparable in presentation quality.

Avoid giving one design an artificial advantage through dramatically better photography, larger apparent garment scale, stronger lifestyle context, more elaborate props, or more premium-looking styling than the rest of the assortment.

Visual variety is acceptable when it supports natural merchandising and gender/style expression, provided the page still reads as one coherent product assortment.

Background context should support the product rather than compete with it. Especially on mobile, keep environmental detail sufficiently quiet or defocused that the garment and printed design remain the unmistakable visual hero.

## Review test

Before approving any standardized LP product image, the Creative Director should be able to answer **yes** to all of the following:

- Does this still look like the intended Printful garment, fabric, and fit?
- Does the garment surface/material read naturally for the intended production blank rather than as a decorative generated texture?
- Is the locked T-shirt design preserved faithfully?
- When compared directly with the locked source, are the print's relative size and placement on the garment materially faithful?
- Are the artwork proportions, wording, typography, illustration, colors, and composition materially faithful to the locked source?
- Are any styling changes presentation-only rather than product changes?
- Is the design easy to inspect and not obscured?
- Is the background sufficiently soft, low-detail, and subordinate that the shirt and print win the first visual read on mobile?
- Does the image avoid crisp or readable background elements that compete with the product?
- Is the image presentation quality broadly fair relative to the other products?
- Would a visitor who liked this image receive substantially the product they thought they were evaluating?

If any answer is no, revise the image before approval.

## Execution approach for MVV #1

Create or approve the standardized primary images one product at a time. Establish the presentation standard with the first one or two representative products before applying it across all 11 products.

For each product, use the exact locked source for its `design_id` as the fidelity reference during image creation and approval. Approved LP images are merchandising derivatives only; they do not supersede the locked creative source.
