# MVV #1 Landing-Page Brand Requirements

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the minimum brand requirements for the Worth the Detour MVV #1 landing page.

The landing page has a different experimental job from Meta acquisition creative.

Its primary question is:

> **Once relevant visitors are paying attention, which actual T-shirt designs do they want?**

The page should therefore confirm the brand/category context efficiently and then give credible products a fair opportunity to earn customer intent.

## 2. Used By / Referenced By

### Primary user

**MVV Landing-Page Developer**
- Uses this document to guide messaging hierarchy, category confirmation, product presentation, and brand consistency.
- Translates these meaning requirements into the simplest effective UX.
- Retains implementation authority over layout, responsive behavior, interaction design, instrumentation, and technical choices.

### Primary collaborator

**Creative Director**
- Uses the product-assortment criteria to identify approved shirts that deserve downstream product testing.
- Supplies executions in a form that can be inspected properly on the LP.
- Should not exclude a strong product merely because it is a weak Meta ad.

### Secondary reference

**Brand Manager**
- Reviews LP copy, product assortment, and the implemented page before launch.

**MVV / Strategy Advisor**
- May reference this document when interpreting downstream product-intent evidence.
- Brand Manager does not own experiment analytics or statistical conclusions.

## 3. Landing-Page Experimental Job

The landing page should allow visitors to move through this diagnostic chain:

> **landing visit → design exposure → design engagement → product-specific intent → lead**

Incoming acquisition context and downstream product interest are different signals.

The LP must not assume:

> incoming `creative_id` = product the visitor should evaluate.

The visitor should be able to encounter and evaluate a broader credible product assortment.

## 4. Minimum Brand Context

A cold or semi-cold visitor should quickly be able to understand:

1. **Worth the Detour is an apparel brand.**
2. **The initial world is antiquing / flea markets / estate sales / vintage discovery / overlooked finds.**
3. **The deeper customer identity is “I see what others miss.”**

This should be accomplished with minimum-sufficient communication.

Do not turn the top of the page into a long brand explanation.

The purpose is:

> **confirm the world → enable product evaluation**

not:

> **re-explain the entire acquisition proposition.**

## 5. Brand Message Hierarchy

### Master brand

> **Worth the Detour**

Creates the broader brand world.

### Identity position

> **For people who see what others miss.**

Explains the deeper customer recognition mechanism.

### Entry-wedge context

Antiques / vintage treasure hunting / flea markets / estate sales / finding overlooked things.

This may be communicated through a concise line, product imagery, collection framing, or another efficient mechanism.

### Products

Actual T-shirt executions should quickly become the main evidence for what the brand means.

## 6. Role of “Antiques Are Worth the Detour”

> **Antiques are worth the detour.**

is a strong expression connecting the master brand to the initial R012 wedge.

It may function as a T-shirt concept, category-decoding copy, acquisition messaging, or collection language.

However, it should not automatically dominate the LP simply because it is strategically elegant. Its actual prominence should depend on the final creative/product system and experimental usefulness.

It does **not** replace:

> **For people who see what others miss.**

The two phrases perform different jobs.

## 7. Product-Assortment Requirement

Landing-page product inclusion should be based primarily on:

> **Is this a credible, desirable Worth the Detour product worth testing?**

not:

> **Did this shirt perform well as a Meta ad?**

The LP assortment may therefore be broader than the acquisition-creative set.

A product that is difficult to comprehend during fast social scrolling may still deserve inclusion.

## 8. Brand-Fit Criteria for Landing-Page Products

A product should express at least one meaningful part of the approved Worth the Detour world.

Relevant mechanisms include:

### Hunter's eye
- noticing;
- discernment;
- spotting overlooked value;
- checking details;
- trusting one's eye.

### Hunt behavior
- one more booth;
- estate-sale routines;
- flea-market rituals;
- detours;
- early starts;
- loss of time;
- inability to skip a promising stop.

### Story of the find
- provenance;
- how something was discovered;
- the absurd logistics of getting it home;
- personal attachment because of the hunt.

### Worth saving
- rescue;
- restoration;
- seeing potential;
- preservation.

### Impractical but necessary
- humorous irrationality around a great find;
- “I have no place for this but obviously I'm buying it” energy.

A product does not need to express every territory.

## 9. Product-Brand Exclusions

Do not include a product merely because it looks “vintage.”

Avoid products whose primary meaning is generic retro aesthetics, generic thrift shopping, generic “old stuff” declaration, rustic/farmhouse clichés, strict antique-age gatekeeping, reseller / side-hustle identity, appraisal authority, generic nostalgia, generic travel, or unrelated discovery themes introduced only because they fit the word “detour.”

## 10. Fair Product-Presentation Requirement

Products must be shown well enough that visitors can actually evaluate them.

This is especially important for products whose message takes a moment to read, illustration contains meaningful detail, visual concept benefits from larger presentation, appeal depends on the relationship between copy and graphic, or wearability is hard to judge from a tiny image.

The LP should not recreate the constraints of a Meta feed.

At the Brand Manager level, the principle is:

> **A credible product deserves a fair presentation before we infer weak product appeal.**

Exact image dimensions, interaction patterns, zoom behavior, responsive treatment, and layout remain the LP developer's responsibility.

## 11. Product Comparison Neutrality

The page should avoid unintentionally giving one product a materially unfair advantage unless that prominence is itself an intentional experiment.

Brand Manager does not require every shirt to receive mathematically identical exposure.

But if product performance will be compared, major presentation asymmetries should be deliberate and understood.

Examples that may distort learning include one design shown dramatically larger without experimental reason, some shirts immediately visible while others are effectively hidden, one product receiving substantially richer contextual explanation, or harder-to-read designs displayed too small to understand.

## 12. Landing-Page Copy Principles

Copy should be observant, curious, knowing without snobbery, lightly obsessive where useful, dry/self-aware where humorous, and appreciative without becoming precious.

Useful language may include find, hunt, estate sale, flea market, antique mall, booth, overlooked, worth a look, good eye, one more booth, detour, hidden gem, weird find, and story of the find.

Avoid fake auction-house formality, generic lifestyle-brand poetry, excessive explanation of the brand philosophy, generic sustainability claims, generic “vintage vibes” language, broad wanderlust language, and invented expertise.

## 13. CTA / Intent Principle

The CTA should support the MVV goal of detecting **real product-specific interest**.

Brand Manager does not prescribe the exact CTA mechanism.

However, the language should not falsely imply that a product is currently purchasable when it is not, a scarcity condition that does not exist, a launch date that has not been committed, or product availability the company cannot fulfill.

MVV #1 does not establish purchase winners because visitors cannot complete a purchase.

## 14. Brand Manager Pre-Launch LP Review

Brand Manager should review the actual implemented page.

### Block / revise if
- a visitor could reasonably misread the business as generic travel or unrelated lifestyle;
- the page primarily communicates vintage aesthetics instead of hunter identity;
- the assortment excludes credible products merely because they are weak social-feed objects;
- significant products cannot be inspected properly;
- copy contradicts Worth the Detour's approved position;
- unrelated future adjacencies have been introduced;
- the page makes claims unsupported by the actual MVV offer.

### Do not block for
- ordinary UX choices within developer authority;
- minor layout preferences;
- visual-style choices owned by Creative Director;
- lack of a complete brand story;
- lack of a mature ecommerce experience;
- absence of features that do not improve MVV learning.

## 15. Success Standard

The LP succeeds at its Brand Manager job if:

> **Visitors quickly know what world they are in, recognize the intended Worth the Detour identity, and then receive a fair opportunity to reveal which actual shirts they want.**

The page should support learning rather than attempt to prove the entire long-term brand.
