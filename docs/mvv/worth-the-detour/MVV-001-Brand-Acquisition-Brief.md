# MVV #1 Brand Acquisition Brief

## 1. Purpose

This brief defines what Worth the Detour acquisition creative must communicate in order to attract **relevant R012 attention**.

It does not prescribe visual execution, ad format, media buying, targeting, or campaign structure.

The acquisition question is:

> **Can an ad communicate something relevant enough to the right treasure-hunting customer, quickly enough in a social scroll, to earn attention and a click?**

Acquisition performance must be evaluated separately from downstream product desirability.

A T-shirt may be a strong product and a weak acquisition creative.

## 2. Used By / Referenced By

### Primary users

**Creative Director**
- Uses this brief to identify which approved concepts are suitable for Meta acquisition creative.
- Uses the recognition territories and guardrails while developing ad executions.
- Does not need to optimize every T-shirt design for Meta-feed readability.

**Performance-marketing / MVV operator**
- Uses this brief to understand what kind of attention the ads are intended to acquire.
- Uses it when interpreting CTR and other acquisition signals.
- Should not interpret the highest-CTR design automatically as the strongest product.

### Secondary reference

**Brand Manager**
- Reviews final acquisition executions against this brief before launch.
- Interprets acquisition findings through the brand lens after MVV.

**MVV Landing-Page Developer**
- May reference this brief to understand what context visitors are likely to receive before arriving on the landing page.
- This brief does not govern landing-page implementation.

## 3. Acquisition Job

The acquisition creative should make enough of the following legible during fast mobile consumption:

1. This is relevant to people who hunt antiques, vintage objects, flea markets, estate sales, salvage, collectibles, or adjacent discovery environments.
2. The message is about the hunter, not merely about old objects.
3. The customer should plausibly recognize part of themselves in the behavior, worldview, or joke.
4. The creative should create enough curiosity or desire to justify learning more.

The ad does **not** need to explain the entire Worth the Detour brand.

## 4. Brand Meaning to Preserve

### Master brand

> **Worth the Detour**

### Positioning

> **For people who see what others miss.**

### Core identity

> **I see what other people miss.**

### Behavioral identity

> **search → notice → evaluate → discover → tell the story of the find**

### Brand center

> **finding + discernment + story**

The customer is not defined merely by owning antiques. The customer gains identity value from finding something interesting, worthwhile, strange, useful, beautiful, historical, or meaningful that another person might overlook.

## 5. Fast-Recognition Acquisition Territories

These are message territories, not finished ads.

### Territory A — The Hunter's Eye

Core recognition:

> **I noticed it before everyone else did.**

Useful material includes spotting overlooked value, checking marks and details, having a good eye, seeing possibility where someone else sees old stuff, and knowing something deserves a second look.

Primary acquisition strength: **identity competence**.

### Territory B — One More Booth

Core recognition:

> **I know exactly how this goes.**

Useful material includes one more booth, one more aisle, one more estate sale, quick stops becoming hours, early mornings, and route changes because there might be something good.

Primary acquisition strength: **fast behavioral recognition + humor**.

### Territory C — Worth the Stop / Worth the Detour

Core recognition:

> **Of course I am stopping. There might be something there.**

Useful material includes antique-mall detours, flea-market signs, estate sales, back-road shops, unexpected stops, and changing plans for a promising hunting opportunity.

Primary acquisition strength: **connects the brand name directly to R012 behavior**.

Guardrail: do not turn this into generic travel, road-trip, or wanderlust messaging.

### Territory D — The Find

Core recognition:

> **You never know until you look.**

Useful material includes overlooked objects, unlikely treasures, strange finds, hidden gems, and the satisfaction of discovering something no one expected.

Primary acquisition strength: **curiosity and discovery**.

Guardrail: do not collapse into generic treasure-hunter clip-art or treasure-chest symbolism.

## 6. Category-Decoding Requirement

Because **Worth the Detour** does not inherently communicate antiques or apparel, the **whole acquisition execution** must carry the entry-wedge meaning.

> **On social, the whole ad is the naming unit—not just the logo.**

An ad may accomplish category decoding through any combination of the product itself, readable shirt copy, supporting ad copy, imagery, setting, customer behavior, caption/headline, or multiple products in a carousel.

There is no requirement that the brand name alone explain the niche.

## 7. Acquisition-Creative Review Criteria

### A. Relevant-attention test
Would the creative disproportionately mean something to the intended treasure-hunting customer rather than merely producing generic curiosity?

### B. Scroll-comprehension test
Can the core idea be understood quickly enough on a small mobile screen?

This is a requirement of the **acquisition execution**, not every T-shirt product.

### C. Recognition test
Could the right customer plausibly think: **“That is me.”**

### D. Category test
Does the execution provide enough context that the viewer can connect it to the antiques / vintage discovery / treasure-hunting world?

### E. Brand-fit test
Does it express some combination of hunt, discernment, discovery, story, insider behavior, or appropriate self-aware humor?

### F. Drift test
Reject or revise if it accidentally positions Worth the Detour as generic vintage aesthetics, thrift fashion, generic nostalgia, rustic/farmhouse merchandise, reseller hustle, appraisal expertise, or travel/wanderlust.

## 8. What Acquisition Creative Does Not Need to Prove

An acquisition execution does **not** need to prove that its featured T-shirt is the most desirable product.

A shirt should not be rejected as a product because its text is harder to read in a fast scroll, its idea benefits from closer inspection, its appeal becomes clearer at larger size, or it does not independently generate strong CTR.

Likewise, a high-CTR creative should not automatically become a product winner.

## 9. Acquisition Interpretation Rule

Use acquisition data primarily to identify:

> **Which creative executions efficiently earn relevant attention?**

Do not automatically infer:

> **Which T-shirt do customers most want?**

Possible outcomes include strong ad + strong product, strong ad + weak product, weak ad + strong product, and weak ad + weak product.

MVV #1 should preserve those distinctions.

## 10. Brand Manager Pre-Launch Review

Brand Manager should review the actual proposed acquisition creatives before launch and block only material problems.

### Block / revise if
- the creative attracts an obviously wrong identity;
- R012 cannot be decoded;
- the creative relies on generic vintage aesthetics rather than hunter identity;
- Worth the Detour is being turned into generic travel;
- the creative contradicts approved brand meaning.

### Do not block for
- minor aesthetic preferences;
- visual choices within Creative Director authority;
- a good product being unsuitable as acquisition creative;
- ordinary experimental uncertainty.

## 11. Success Standard

The acquisition system succeeds at its Brand Manager job if:

> **The right kind of customer can quickly recognize enough of their world or identity to want to click and learn more.**

Whether the featured product ultimately becomes a desirable T-shirt is a separate downstream question.
