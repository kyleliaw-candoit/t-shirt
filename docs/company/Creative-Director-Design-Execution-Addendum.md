# Creative Director Design / Execution Addendum

## Purpose

This is a lightweight methodology amendment created from actual T-shirt design execution. It applies alongside the current Creative Director Methodology and captures the smallest durable process changes that improved product quality.

It does **not** replace the full methodology and should not become a large parallel operating system.

## 1. Evaluate Two Different Things

Every T-shirt concept should be judged on two independent axes:

### Concept Strength

Does the idea create recognition, identity, humor, meaning, belonging, desire, or another reason to wear it?

### T-shirt Strength

Independent of strategic cleverness, is the actual shirt visually desirable, well composed, wearable, and appropriate to the garment?

Use this distinction diagnostically rather than as a mechanical score.

- strong concept + strong execution: advance,
- strong concept + weak execution: redesign before drawing strategic conclusions,
- modest concept + exceptional execution: may still be a commercially strong product,
- weak concept + weak execution: retire.

> **A strategically correct concept with a weak T-shirt execution is still a Creative Director failure.**

## 2. Start With the Shirt Architecture

Before asking, “What illustration represents this message?”, ask:

> **What kind of T-shirt should this idea become?**

Choose the intended graphic architecture, scale, hierarchy, typography role, palette relationship, texture logic, and garment relationship before overdeveloping illustration.

Useful architectures are maintained in the T-shirt Design Playbook rather than expanded here.

## 3. Treat the Garment as Part of the Design

The design is not floating artwork placed onto a blank.

Garment color, silhouette, print footprint, visual center, ink relationship, and intended production treatment are part of the product.

For controlled MVV, garment and presentation conditions may intentionally be held constant. That testing constraint does not remove the Creative Director's responsibility to design for the actual garment.

## 4. Design Preflight

Before showing a design for founder review, the Creative Director should be able to answer:

1. **Recognition** — why would the intended customer want to wear this?
2. **Architecture** — what graphic structure is this using, and why?
3. **Hierarchy** — what reads first from a distance, and what rewards a closer look?
4. **Typography role** — is the type acting as voice, label, artifact, institution, headline, annotation, or another intentional role?
5. **Scale** — why is the graphic this size emotionally and compositionally?
6. **Owning color** — which color visually owns the design?
7. **Texture reason** — what is aged or textured, and why?
8. **Garment relationship** — does this feel designed for this shirt rather than pasted onto it?
9. **Wearability** — would the shirt remain desirable if the customer cared slightly less about the concept?
10. **Edit** — what can be removed without weakening the idea?

This is a qualitative preflight, not a scoring matrix.

## 5. One Design at a Time

When a concept is moving through detailed execution:

1. develop one design,
2. founder reviews it,
3. refine only that design,
4. founder approves it,
5. archive and verify the exact approved visual,
6. mark it `LOCKED`,
7. then move to the next design.

This rule exists because parallel iteration can cause already-good designs to drift or be accidentally regenerated.

## 6. Learning Discipline

Distinguish durable craft knowledge from opportunity-specific evidence.

- reusable T-shirt craft guidance belongs in the **Creative Director T-shirt Design Playbook**,
- opportunity-specific lessons belong with that opportunity's creative records,
- market evidence should override aesthetic habit when repeated credible results justify a change.

Do not convert one successful design choice into a universal rule without evidence.

## Stopping Rule

This addendum is sufficient when it improves the quality and consistency of real T-shirt execution without adding bureaucracy.

Add more process only when repeated execution demonstrates a concrete need.
