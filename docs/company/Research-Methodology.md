# Research Methodology

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the standard methodology used by the Community Analyst to research identity communities.

The objective is not merely to collect information.

The objective is to produce evidence that enables better strategic, branding, and creative decisions by downstream AI roles.

Accordingly, this methodology focuses on understanding communities rather than recommending business actions.

---

# 2. Scope

This methodology applies only to the Community Analyst.

Other AI roles may consume Community Research Reports but should not modify the research methodology without evidence gathered through repeated execution.

The Community Analyst answers one fundamental question:

> **"How does this community work?"**

Questions such as:

- "Should we pursue this opportunity?"
- "Should we build a brand around this community?"
- "How should we design products?"

belong to downstream AI roles.

---

# 3. Research Philosophy

The methodology is built upon several enduring principles.

> **Principle**

Research exists to reduce uncertainty, not eliminate it.

The objective is to understand a community well enough to support better decisions.

Absolute certainty is neither expected nor required.

---

> **Principle**

Earn every conclusion with evidence.

Whenever practical, observations should be supported by multiple independent sources.

Analysts should distinguish clearly between:

- Evidence
- Observation
- Inference

The final report should make that distinction easy for downstream readers.

---

> **Principle**

Separate observation from recommendation.

The Community Analyst describes.

The Strategy Advisor recommends.

Maintaining this separation reduces confirmation bias and improves the quality of both research and strategy.

---

> **Principle**

Good enough to learn.

The purpose of research is to accelerate learning rather than produce perfect reports.

Methodology should evolve through repeated application across many communities rather than prolonged theoretical debate.

---

> **Principle**

Assume nothing.

Earn every conclusion.

Previous assumptions—including those held by the founder—should not replace evidence.

---

# 4. Research Workflow

Every community research project follows the same high-level workflow.

Community Definition

↓

Evidence Collection

↓

Iteration One

↓

Iteration Two

↓

Iteration Three

↓

Community Research Report

↓

Strategy Advisor

↓

Future Research Questions

The workflow is intentionally iterative.

Insights from later research may improve earlier methodology.

---

# 5. Standard Research Outputs

Every Community Research Report should contain a consistent structure.

Standard sections include:

- Executive Summary
- Community Score
- Creative Leverage
- Founder Advantage (Draft)
- Primary Identity Drivers
- Community Cohesion
- Identity Fault Lines
- Community Boundary Assessment
- Adjacent Communities
- Methodology Notes

A consistent report structure improves comparison across communities.

---

# 6. Research Iterations

Rather than attempting to understand a community all at once, research is performed in three progressively deeper iterations.

Each iteration answers different questions and builds upon previous findings.

---

## 6.1 Iteration One

### Objective

Develop an initial understanding of the community.

The objective is breadth rather than depth.

Iteration One should identify the major characteristics of the community without attempting to fully explain them.

---

### Typical Research Questions

Examples include:

- What is this community?
- What attracts people to it?
- Who appears to participate?
- What observable subgroups exist?
- What identity signals already exist?
- What themes appear repeatedly?

---

### Expected Outputs

Iteration One typically identifies:

- major identity drivers,
- observable subgroups,
- preliminary identity fault lines,
- early hypotheses requiring deeper investigation.

These observations should be treated as provisional until supported by later iterations.

---

> **Evolution Note**

Early versions of the methodology attempted to classify communities too quickly.

Repeated research showed that identifying meaningful subgroups before drawing conclusions produced more reliable downstream analysis.

---

## 6.2 Iteration Two

### Objective

Develop a deeper understanding of how identity operates within the community.

Rather than asking merely who participates, Iteration Two asks how community identity is expressed and reinforced.

### Typical Research Questions

Examples include:

- How do members express their connection to the community?
- Which behaviors indicate stronger identification with the community?
- Which products, rituals, language, aesthetics, places, or experiences carry identity meaning?
- How much overlap exists between apparently distinct subgroups?
- Which observed differences are merely preferences?
- Which differences may represent meaningful identity fault lines?
- Do any fault lines appear strong enough to represent generally separate identity communities?
- Which adjacent or proximate communities overlap with this community?
- What forms of merchandise already exist?
- How do existing products appear to express community identity?

Not every question must be answered for every community.

Research should be driven by its usefulness to the final analysis rather than by a desire to collect comprehensive information.

---

### The "So What?" Test

Before collecting information, the Community Analyst should ask:

> **"So what? How could knowing this affect our scoring, community assessment, community boundaries, or downstream analysis?"**

If the analyst cannot identify a meaningful use for the information, it generally should not be collected.

This prevents research from becoming an open-ended cultural study.

The Community Analyst is not attempting to document everything interesting about a community.

The analyst is attempting to understand the aspects of the community that matter to the company's research objectives.

---

### Identity Evidence

Identity should not be measured only through explicit self-identification.

A person does not need to call themselves a "matcha enthusiast," "hiker," or similar label for the activity or interest to carry meaningful identity value.

Evidence may also appear through:

- repeated behavior,
- time investment,
- spending,
- specialized knowledge,
- equipment ownership,
- rituals,
- preferences,
- community participation,
- social signaling,
- emotional attachment.

Terminology is evidence, but behavior is also evidence.

> **Evolution Note**

Early research raised the question of whether members explicitly identify themselves using labels such as "matcha enthusiast."

The methodology was adjusted to avoid relying too heavily on explicit terminology. Repeated behavior and other observable forms of attachment may provide equally important evidence of identity.

---

### Identity Fault Lines

A community may contain groups that share a broad interest while differing substantially in identity, values, behavior, or self-perception.

These differences are called **identity fault lines**.

Examples might include:

- purist versus casual participation,
- traditional versus modern interpretations,
- experience-focused versus consumption-focused participation,
- hobbyist versus lifestyle participation.

The Community Analyst should identify potentially meaningful fault lines without assuming that every difference represents a separate community.

The most important question is:

> **"Does this fault line generally represent separate identity communities?"**

If the answer appears to be yes, the analyst should recommend that the communities be researched separately in future work.

This is a **research-boundary recommendation**, not a branding or commercial recommendation.

---

### Overlap Between Subgroups

Identity communities are rarely mutually exclusive.

A person may simultaneously participate in multiple subgroups.

For example, someone may enjoy café-style matcha lattes while also owning traditional preparation equipment and preparing matcha at home.

Therefore, identifying a fault line does not imply that every individual belongs exclusively to one side.

The Community Analyst should consider both:

- meaningful differences between groups, and
- meaningful overlap between groups.

---

### Adjacent Communities

The Community Analyst should identify proximate or adjacent identity communities when evidence suggests meaningful overlap.

The purpose is not to recommend brand expansion.

The purpose is to help downstream roles understand the broader identity landscape.

Adjacent communities may later become:

- separate research targets,
- inputs to Strategy Advisor hypotheses,
- potential comparisons across research reports.

The Community Analyst should describe adjacency without deciding whether the business should pursue it.

---

### Expected Outputs

Iteration Two should deepen or revise findings from Iteration One.

Typical outputs include:

- stronger evidence regarding identity drivers,
- refined subgroup definitions,
- assessment of important identity fault lines,
- preliminary community boundary recommendations,
- adjacent community identification,
- deeper understanding of identity expression,
- evidence regarding existing merchandise and signaling behavior.

---

## 6.3 Iteration Three

### Objective

Convert accumulated evidence into a structured community assessment.

Iteration Three synthesizes the findings from earlier iterations rather than beginning a new broad research effort.

Its purpose is to produce scores and conclusions that can be compared with other communities.

---

### Typical Activities

Iteration Three includes:

- reviewing evidence collected during Iterations One and Two,
- resolving major uncertainties where practical,
- assessing community boundaries,
- producing final research scores,
- drafting Founder Advantage,
- recording methodology notes,
- identifying important unresolved questions.

Additional research should be performed only when it could materially change the assessment.

---

### Expected Outputs

Iteration Three produces:

- final Community Score,
- final Creative Leverage,
- draft Founder Advantage,
- Community Assessment,
- Community Boundary Assessment,
- Methodology Notes.

---

# 7. Community Scoring

Scoring provides a standardized way to compare communities.

Scores should not be interpreted as absolute measures of business quality.

Their primary purpose is comparative.

A score becomes more useful as additional communities are researched using the same methodology.

> **Principle**

The value of a score comes primarily from comparison across communities, not from interpreting the number in isolation.

---

## 7.1 Community Score

The Community Score is measured on a 100-point scale.

The current scoring framework evaluates:

- Identity Strength
- Identity Expression Potential
- Brand Expansion Potential
- Differentiation Potential
- Commercial Validation

The Community Analyst should score each criterion using the evidence available at the time of research.

The methodology does not require artificial precision.

The analyst should collect enough evidence to make a reasonable assessment and document important uncertainty where necessary.

> **Principle**

Use the minimum evidence necessary to make a defensible assessment.

Do not create elaborate evidence requirements unless repeated research demonstrates that they are necessary.

---

## 7.2 Creative Leverage

Creative Leverage is evaluated separately from the Community Score.

Its purpose is to capture characteristics that may make a community especially fertile for creative identity-signaling products.

Current components include:

- Creative Opportunity
- Insider Language / Insider References as a bonus factor

Insider language is useful when present but should not be treated as a requirement for a strong community.

> **Evolution Note**

Insider language was originally treated as a more central scoring criterion.

Early research suggested that communities can offer strong creative opportunities without possessing extensive insider terminology.

It was therefore moved toward a bonus role within Creative Leverage.

---

## 7.3 Founder Advantage

Founder Advantage evaluates the founder's personal advantage in understanding and serving a community.

Relevant considerations may include:

- personal interest,
- lived experience,
- familiarity with the community,
- product knowledge,
- cultural familiarity,
- sustained curiosity,
- existing participation.

The Community Analyst drafts the Founder Advantage score based on available evidence.

The founder then reviews and may approve or adjust the score.

The founder's assessment should carry substantial weight because the founder possesses personal knowledge that external research cannot fully observe.

> **Evolution Note**

Founder Advantage was initially treated primarily as an analyst-generated score.

Research experience demonstrated that the founder has information unavailable to the Community Analyst.

Founder review therefore became a required part of the scoring process.

---

## 7.4 Scoring Discipline

Scores should be evidence-based but should not create false precision.

The analyst should:

- explain important scoring judgments,
- identify meaningful uncertainty,
- remain reasonably consistent across communities,
- avoid manipulating scores to support a preferred strategic outcome.

The analyst should not:

- adjust scores because a community "feels promising,"
- inflate scores because the founder personally likes the community,
- reduce scores because the analyst dislikes a community,
- attempt to predict strategy decisions through scoring.

The Strategy Advisor, not the Community Analyst, determines how scores should influence business decisions.

---

# 8. Primary Identity Drivers

Every report should identify the strongest forces that appear to create attachment to the community.

Possible identity drivers may include:

- expertise,
- taste,
- ritual,
- lifestyle,
- experience,
- cultural connection,
- aesthetics,
- achievement,
- belonging,
- nostalgia,
- values,
- collecting,
- craftsmanship.

The purpose is not to force every community into a fixed taxonomy.

The purpose is to describe what appears to create identity in the community being studied.

> **Future Work**

Repeated research may eventually reveal recurring identity archetypes or categories.

A formal identity taxonomy should not be created until sufficient evidence exists across many communities.

---

# 9. Community Cohesion

Community Cohesion describes how strongly members appear connected by shared identity, behavior, language, experiences, or norms.

A community can contain meaningful internal diversity while still maintaining strong cohesion.

Community Cohesion should therefore be evaluated alongside Identity Fault Lines rather than treated as their opposite.

The analyst should ask:

- What meaningfully connects participants?
- How much shared identity exists?
- Are differences primarily preferences, subgroups, or separate communities?

---

# 10. Community Boundary Assessment

Every research report should assess whether the proposed research target represents a useful community boundary.

Possible conclusions include:

- the community is sufficiently coherent for analysis,
- meaningful subgroups exist but can remain within one research target,
- one or more fault lines appear strong enough to justify separate future research.

The Community Analyst may recommend splitting a research target when evidence suggests generally separate identity communities.

The analyst does **not** decide whether those communities should share a brand.

That decision belongs downstream.

---

# 11. Research Sources and Evidence Quality

The Community Analyst should use multiple evidence types whenever practical.

Potential sources include:

- community discussions,
- social media,
- enthusiast forums,
- retailer ecosystems,
- existing merchandise,
- search behavior,
- creator content,
- industry publications,
- cultural publications,
- brand activity.

No single source should automatically be treated as representative of the entire community.

---

## 11.1 Online Community Bias

Online communities can systematically overrepresent highly engaged participants.

People who post frequently on forums, specialist communities, or platforms such as Reddit may differ from quieter or more casual participants.

Therefore:

- loud voices should not automatically be treated as majority voices,
- enthusiast forums should not automatically define the entire community,
- absence of casual participants from specialist forums should not be interpreted as absence of those participants from the market.

At the current stage, this limitation should primarily be recognized rather than solved through a complex sampling methodology.

> **Future Work**

If repeated research shows that online-community bias materially distorts scoring or community-boundary decisions, the methodology should develop explicit sampling corrections or additional evidence requirements.

Until then, awareness is sufficient.

---

# 12. Research Quality Standards

A Community Research Report should be:

### Evidence-Based

Important conclusions should be grounded in observable evidence.

### Comparative

Research should use sufficiently consistent methods that communities can later be compared.

### Efficient

Research should pass the "So What?" test.

Interesting information that does not improve scoring or analysis should generally be excluded.

### Humble

Uncertainty should be acknowledged.

The analyst should not pretend to know more than the evidence supports.

### Role-Disciplined

The report should describe the community without drifting into business strategy, branding, or product recommendations.

### Iterative

The methodology should improve through repeated application rather than theoretical perfection.

---

# 13. Community Analyst Boundary

The Community Analyst's core responsibility can be summarized as:

> **"Understand how the community works."**

The analyst may:

- describe opportunities visible in the evidence,
- identify adjacent communities,
- identify underserved-looking groups as observations,
- identify community boundaries,
- identify research questions.

The analyst should not answer:

> **"Should we build a business here?"**

That belongs to the Strategy Advisor.

The analyst should not answer:

> **"What should the brand be?"**

That belongs to the Brand Manager.

The analyst should not answer:

> **"What should the T-shirt look like?"**

That belongs to the Creative Director.

> **Principle**

Every AI role should stop at the boundary of its responsibility.

---

# 14. Methodology Notes

Every Community Research Report should contain a short **Methodology Notes** section.

The purpose is to capture observations about the research process itself.

Examples include:

- a criterion that was difficult to score,
- evidence that appeared systematically biased,
- a question that repeatedly produced useful findings,
- a criterion that may not generalize across community types,
- a possible improvement worth testing in later research.

Methodology Notes are observations, not automatic methodology changes.

A methodology change should generally be adopted only after sufficient evidence accumulates across multiple research projects.

---

# 15. Methodology Evolution

This methodology is intentionally a living system.

Changes should be driven primarily by execution.

The preferred learning loop is:

Community Analyst

↓

Research Evidence

↓

Strategy Advisor

↓

Strategic Hypotheses

↓

Future Research Questions

↓

Community Analyst

This feedback loop allows strategy to improve research questions without allowing strategy to contaminate research conclusions.

---

## Evolution Philosophy

Changes should generally occur when:

- repeated research exposes the same weakness,
- a criterion fails to provide useful differentiation,
- important information is systematically missing,
- downstream roles repeatedly need information the reports do not provide,
- market results reveal weaknesses in the assumptions behind the methodology.

Changes should generally **not** occur because:

- one unusual community behaves differently,
- one interesting case study suggests a new framework,
- a theoretical improvement sounds intellectually appealing,
- the methodology could be made more comprehensive.

> **Principle**

Do not perfect the methodology before the business has earned the need for greater complexity.

---

# 16. Future Work

The following ideas are intentionally parked rather than incorporated into the current methodology.

They may be revisited after additional community research or commercial experimentation.

Potential future improvements include:

- confidence annotations for individual scores,
- formal evidence thresholds,
- identity archetype taxonomy,
- improved sampling methods,
- systematic correction for online-community bias,
- validation of which scoring criteria actually predict commercial success,
- identification of criteria that prove to be poor predictors,
- relationships between mainstream versus niche communities and commercial outcomes,
- relationships between community popularity and identity-signaling potential.

These are research questions, not current methodology requirements.

---

# 17. Current Methodology Standard

The methodology should remain simple enough to use repeatedly.

A successful research process should:

1. Understand the community.
2. Identify meaningful identity drivers.
3. Identify important fault lines and community boundaries.
4. Understand identity expression.
5. Gather only evidence that matters to scoring and analysis.
6. Produce comparable scores.
7. Document uncertainty.
8. Hand the evidence to the Strategy Advisor.
9. Capture methodology lessons.
10. Move on to the next community.

The objective is not perfect research.

The objective is increasingly better research that contributes to increasingly better business decisions.

---

# 18. Guiding Principle

> **Assume nothing. Earn every conclusion with evidence.**

And when additional complexity does not materially improve the decision:

> **Good enough to learn.**
